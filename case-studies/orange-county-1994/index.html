Orange County, 1994

Leverage and interest rate risk; collateral calls
A conservative bond portfolio, borrowed three times over.

At a glance

When1994
WhoThe Orange County investment pool, serving about 200 local public bodies
InstrumentsHigh-grade agency notes and structured notes such as inverse floaters, leveraged with repo
LossAbout $1.7bn and a Chapter 9 bankruptcy
Risk typeLeverage and interest rate risk; collateral calls

What happened

Orange County ran a pooled fund for itself and roughly two hundred schools, cities and districts. The securities were high quality, mostly agency paper, but the pool borrowed against them through repurchase agreements until it held about $20bn of securities on around $7.5bn of deposits. Much of what it bought also had leverage written into the coupon. When US rates rose through 1994 the portfolio lost about $1.7bn, lenders demanded more collateral, and in December the county filed for bankruptcy, then the largest municipal failure in US history.

Why it went wrong

There were two layers of leverage, and rising rates hit both. Repo multiplied every price move by nearly three. Inverse floaters, whose coupons fall as short rates rise, behave like much longer bonds than their maturity suggests. As prices fell the repo lenders called for collateral, forcing sales into a falling market. And because the pool reported at book value rather than market value, the depositors had no warning until it was too late.

What it teaches

Credit quality and market risk are separate questions: a portfolio of safe issuers can still be a highly risky position once it is leveraged. Leverage hidden inside a coupon formula will not show up in any measure that only counts borrowing. Public funds now face leverage limits, maturity limits and mark-to-market reporting because of this case.

Key terms

Repurchase agreement
Selling a security for cash with an agreement to buy it back, the standard way to borrow against bonds.
Inverse floater
A note whose coupon falls as a benchmark rate rises, giving it far more interest rate risk than its maturity suggests.
Effective duration
The true price sensitivity of a structured note to rates, which can be several times its stated maturity.
Collateral call
A repo lender’s demand for more security as the collateral falls, met by selling into the same fall.
Book value reporting
Reporting holdings at cost rather than market, which conceals a loss until it must be realised.
Investment pool
A shared fund holding cash for several public bodies, whose participants here could not see the risk taken.
