Daiwa Bank, 1995

Operational risk: concealment and late disclosure
Eleven years of losses, hidden by the man who kept the records.

At a glance

WhenMid-1980s to 1995
WhoA bond trader at Daiwa Bank's New York branch
InstrumentsUS Treasury bonds, with securities sold out of custody to cover losses
LossAbout $1.1bn, a $340m fine and the end of Daiwa's US business
Risk typeOperational risk: concealment and late disclosure

What happened

A trader at Daiwa Bank's New York branch lost about $1.1bn trading US Treasuries over eleven years. He had started in the branch's back office and kept control of custody and settlement after moving to trading. To cover his losses he sold bonds belonging to the bank and its customers, then forged the custody statements that should have shown them. After roughly thirty thousand unauthorised trades he confessed in a letter to the bank's president in July 1995.

Why it went wrong

The custody records that were supposed to prove the securities existed were produced by the person who had sold them, and nobody checked them directly against the outside custodian's statements. Then came a second failure: after the confession, Daiwa waited about two months before telling US regulators. The Federal Reserve treated that delay as the more serious offence and ordered the bank out of the United States within ninety days.

What it teaches

The person who trades must never also confirm, settle or hold the custody records. Reconciliation has to go to an outside source such as the custodian or counterparty. And once a loss is found, how quickly and honestly it is disclosed matters as much as its size.

Key terms

Separation of duties
Keeping trading, confirmation, settlement and custody in different hands so no one person can conceal a trade.
Custody
Holding securities on behalf of an owner, with records that are meant to prove they exist.
Independent reconciliation
Agreeing internal records to a source outside the unit, such as a custodian or counterparty statement.
Unauthorised trading
Trades outside a trader’s mandate or limits, often concealed by falsified records.
Regulatory disclosure
A bank’s obligation to tell its supervisors promptly about material losses or misconduct.
Back office
The operations that confirm, settle and record trades after the front office executes them.
