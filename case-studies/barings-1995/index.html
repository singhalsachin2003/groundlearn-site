Barings, 1995

Operational risk: rogue trading and missing segregation of duties
One trader, both sides of his own desk.

At a glance

When1995
WhoBarings' Singapore futures desk
InstrumentsNikkei 225 futures and short straddles on the index
LossAbout £827m, more than the bank's capital
Risk typeOperational risk: rogue trading and missing segregation of duties

What happened

Barings' Singapore office was meant to run low-risk arbitrage, buying Nikkei futures on one exchange and selling them on another for tiny price differences. Instead a single trader built a large, hidden directional position. After the Kobe earthquake on 17 January 1995 the Nikkei fell sharply, and the position was increased rather than cut. By late February the losses were larger than the bank's entire capital, and the 233-year-old firm was sold to ING for one pound.

Why it went wrong

The trader controlled both the trading and the settlement of his own book, so the records of what he had done were produced by him. Losses sat in an error account outside the reported numbers. Head office kept sending hundreds of millions to meet margin calls without reconciling what that money was paying for. Underneath were an outright long futures position and short straddles: a bet that the index would stay in a range, at a size nobody in London knew existed.

What it teaches

Profits that do not fit the strategy are a warning, not a success: a near-riskless arbitrage cannot produce large, steady gains. Money leaving the firm for margin can always be checked against positions. Separate front and back offices, mandatory leave and independent reconciliation became standard because all of them were missing here.

Key terms

Segregation of duties
Keeping trading, confirmation and settlement in separate hands so no one person can both trade and record.
Error account
An account for correcting mistaken bookings, and here the place concealed losses were parked.
Short straddle
Selling a call and a put at the same strike: premium now, unbounded loss if the market moves far.
Index arbitrage
Trading the same index future on two exchanges for a small price difference, with little directional risk.
Reconciliation
Checking the firm’s records against the exchange’s and the cash actually paid — the control that was absent.
Operational risk
Loss from failed processes, people or systems, as distinct from market or credit risk.
