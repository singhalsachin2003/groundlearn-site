Sumitomo copper, 1996

Operational and concentration risk; market manipulation
One trader, a grip on world copper, and ten years of hidden losses.

At a glance

WhenMid-1980s to 1996
WhoSumitomo Corporation's chief copper trader
InstrumentsLondon Metal Exchange copper futures and physical metal
LossAbout $2.6bn
Risk typeOperational and concentration risk; market manipulation

What happened

For a decade Sumitomo Corporation's head copper trader was one of the most powerful figures in the world copper market, holding large positions in LME futures and warehoused metal. He was also hiding losses that went back to the 1980s, using unauthorised trades and accounts kept off the company's books. A tight market and steep backwardation in 1995 drew complaints and regulatory attention. Sumitomo announced the unauthorised trading in June 1996, and the loss eventually reached about $2.6bn.

Why it went wrong

Holding enough of the nearby futures and deliverable metal lets a dominant long keep prices above where supply and demand would put them, which looks profitable as long as the position is held. But a position that props up the market can only be sold into a falling one. Concealment worked because one person controlled the trading, the records and the broker relationships. Once he was moved off the desk, the positions had to be unwound and copper fell sharply.

What it teaches

Concentration risk can sit in a person as well as a position. A market can be held away from its natural level for a long time, which makes a dominant position look good right up until the exit. The LME's rules on dominant positions, which require large holders to lend metal back at capped premiums, date from this case.

Key terms

Dominant position
A holding of futures and deliverable stock large enough to influence the price and spreads of a market.
Backwardation
Nearby prices above later ones, often a sign that deliverable supply is scarce.
Squeeze
Holders of short positions forced to buy or pay to roll because deliverable supply is controlled by others.
Off-book account
A trading account kept outside the firm’s official records, where losses can be hidden.
Lending rule
An exchange requirement that a dominant holder lend metal to the market at a capped premium.
Key-person risk
Dependence on one individual whose absence, error or misconduct the organisation cannot absorb.
