Case studies: 22 market failures explained

Twenty-two failures, in the order they happened, from Metallgesellschaft in 1993 to Credit Suisse in 2023. Each one is a mechanism worth understanding, and many of them were a position that was sound until it had to be funded.

The 1990s

  • 1993
    MetallgesellschaftThe right hedge, on the wrong clock. Funding liquidity and hedge basis risk.
  • 1994
    Bankers Trust and P&GA cheaper loan that was really a sold option. Model and valuation risk; mis-selling.
  • 1994
    Orange CountyA conservative bond portfolio, borrowed three times over. Leverage and interest rate risk; collateral calls.
  • 1995
    BaringsOne trader, both sides of his own desk. Operational risk: rogue trading and missing segregation of duties.
  • 1995
    Daiwa BankEleven years of losses, hidden by the man who kept the records. Operational risk: concealment and late disclosure.
  • 1996
    Sumitomo copperOne trader, a grip on world copper, and ten years of hidden losses. Operational and concentration risk; market manipulation.
  • 1998
    LTCMConvergence trades, twenty-five times over. Liquidity and model risk; leverage and crowded trades.
  • 1999
    Ashanti GoldfieldsSeven years of production, sold forward. Hedging, collateral and funding liquidity risk.

The 2000s

  • 2001
    EnronHedges against itself, and loans dressed as trades. Accounting, counterparty and wrong-way risk.
  • 2002
    Allied Irish and AllfirstFake options, real losses, and rates the trader supplied himself. Operational risk: fictitious trades and unconfirmed deals.
  • 2006
    AmaranthA spread trade too large to leave. Market liquidity and concentration risk.
  • 2008
    Société GénéraleFictitious hedges, and a €4.9bn exit. Operational risk: rogue trading and the cost of the unwind.
  • 2008
    Lehman BrothersNine hundred thousand trades, closed out at once. Counterparty risk, close-out netting and central clearing.
  • 2008
    AIGProtection that could not pay its collateral calls. Wrong-way risk, collateral calls and rating triggers.

The 2010s

  • 2011
    MF GlobalA broker’s bet on euro bonds, and its clients’ missing money. Funding and liquidity risk; client money segregation.
  • 2012
    The London WhaleA hedge that grew into the market it was hedging. Market and model risk; basis risk and position size.
  • 2015
    The Swiss franc floorA guarantee withdrawn in a morning. Gap risk, suppressed volatility and leverage.

The 2020s

  • 2021
    ArchegosFive banks, one position, and none of them could see it. Counterparty, concentration and margin risk in prime brokerage.
  • 2022
    The LME nickel squeezeA hedge that could not be delivered into. Basis risk, margin calls and market closure.
  • 2022
    The gilt LDI crisisPension hedges that worked, and could not be funded. Leverage, collateral calls and funding liquidity risk.
  • 2023
    Silicon Valley BankLong bonds, unhedged, funded by deposits that could leave in a day. Interest rate risk in the banking book and deposit run risk.
  • 2023
    Credit Suisse AT1Bondholders wiped out while shareholders were paid. Contingent capital and contract (trigger) risk.

By theme

Funding and collateral: hedges that could not be paid for

Metallgesellschaft, Ashanti Goldfields, AIG, The LME nickel squeeze, The gilt LDI crisis, MF Global.

Rogue trading and missing controls

Barings, Daiwa Bank, Sumitomo copper, Allied Irish and Allfirst, Société Générale.

Leverage, liquidity and crowded positions

Orange County, LTCM, Amaranth, The London Whale, Archegos, Silicon Valley Bank.

Contracts, counterparties and sudden breaks

Bankers Trust and P&G, Enron, Lehman Brothers, The Swiss franc floor, Credit Suisse AT1.
