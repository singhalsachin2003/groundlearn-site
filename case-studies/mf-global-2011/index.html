MF Global, 2011

Funding and liquidity risk; client money segregation
A broker’s bet on euro bonds, and its clients’ missing money.

At a glance

When2011
WhoMF Global, a large US futures broker
InstrumentsAbout $6.3bn of short-dated euro-area government bonds financed with repo-to-maturity
LossBankruptcy on 31 October 2011; about $1.6bn of client money missing
Risk typeFunding and liquidity risk; client money segregation

What happened

In its final year, futures broker MF Global started trading for its own account, buying about $6.3bn of short-dated Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Irish and Belgian government bonds and betting they would be repaid at maturity. As the euro crisis deepened, its regulator demanded more capital against the positions, haircuts and margin calls rose, and in late October 2011 it reported a loss and was downgraded. Clients pulled out, and within a week it was bankrupt with about $1.6bn missing from accounts meant to hold customers' money separately.

Why it went wrong

The bonds were financed with repo-to-maturity, which the accounting rules of the time treated as a sale. Both the bonds and the borrowing left the balance sheet, and the expected profit was booked up front, so the position's size was hard to see. But the credit risk stayed with the firm, and so did the obligation to post margin if the bonds fell. In the final days, customer segregated money was used to meet the firm's own obligations.

What it teaches

Client money is the line a broker must never cross, and segregation has to be reconciled daily, especially under stress. Accounting treatment does not change economic exposure. Accounting standards were later changed to treat repo-to-maturity as secured borrowing, and US customer fund rules were tightened. Futures customers eventually recovered their money through the bankruptcy.

Key terms

Repo-to-maturity
A repurchase agreement that runs until the underlying bond matures, so the financing never needs rolling.
Segregation
Keeping customer money and assets apart from the broker’s own, so they are not available to its creditors.
Haircut
The discount applied to a security’s value when it is used as collateral for borrowing.
Derecognition
Removing an asset from the balance sheet because accounting rules treat it as sold.
Futures commission merchant
A US broker that holds customer money for futures trading and must keep it segregated.
Principal risk
Risk a firm takes with its own capital, as opposed to acting as agent for its clients.
