Duration and convexity: how good is the approximation?

A ten-year bond repriced at four yield shocks, comparing the exact price change with the duration estimate and the duration-plus-convexity estimate.

The two formulas

Duration is a first-order estimate of how a bond's price changes when its yield changes. Convexity adds the second-order term, which corrects for the fact that the price-yield curve is bent, not straight.

ΔP / P ≈ − Dmod × Δy
ΔP / P ≈ − Dmod × Δy + ½ × C × (Δy)²

Modified duration is Macaulay duration divided by (1 + y), where y is the yield per period. Convexity is the second derivative of price with respect to yield, divided by price. Both are measured with Δy as a decimal, so 100 bp is 0.01.

Worked example

A 10-year bond pays a 5% annual coupon and yields 5%, so it prices at par, 100.00. Its measures are:

  • Macaulay duration: 8.1078 years
  • Modified duration: 7.7217
  • Convexity: 75.00

For a +100 bp shock the duration estimate is −7.7217 × 0.01 × 100 = −7.72. The convexity term adds ½ × 75.00 × 0.01² × 100 = +0.37, giving −7.35. Repricing the bond at 6% gives the exact figure. The table compares all four shocks (price change per 100 of par):

Yield shockExactDuration onlyDuration + convexity
+100 bp−7.36−7.72−7.35
−100 bp+8.11+7.72+8.10
+200 bp−14.05−15.44−13.94
−200 bp+17.06+15.44+16.94

What the table shows

  • Duration alone is symmetric. It gives the same size of gain for a fall in yields as the loss for a rise. The real price moves are not symmetric.
  • Duration overstates losses and understates gains. For a +200 bp shock it says −15.44 while the true change is −14.05. For −200 bp it says +15.44 while the true change is +17.06.
  • Convexity closes most of the gap. Adding it moves both estimates close to the exact figures, and the remaining error grows with the size of the shock (third-order terms).
  • Positive convexity is good for the holder. For the same duration, a more convex bond loses less when yields rise and gains more when they fall. That is why investors pay for it.

Common exam traps

  • Mixing percent and decimal: 100 bp is 0.01 in the formula, so (Δy)² is 0.0001, not 1.
  • Using Macaulay duration where the question gives modified duration, or the reverse. Modified is the one that links price to yield directly.
  • Forgetting the ½ in front of the convexity term.
  • Assuming callable bonds or mortgage-backed securities have positive convexity. Embedded options can make convexity negative, so the duration-plus-convexity estimate is built differently (effective duration and effective convexity, from repricing in a model).
  • Treating duration as a time measure only. It is both a weighted-average time to cash flows and a price sensitivity.
